Makers

Flour from the old mill

After forty years of silence, the water wheel at Paragon Mill is turning again, and the flour it grinds is already on bakers’ shelves from Salisbury to Truro.

Words Yusuf Nwosu · Photographs Declan Burrows

The first thing you notice is the sound. A low, steady rumble comes up through the floorboards of Paragon Mill, where two French burr stones have been turning since the first week of October, and Leah Pritchard, the miller, can tell by ear when the grain is feeding too fast. “It changes pitch,” she says, one hand flat on the hopper. “You learn to listen before you look.”

The mill stood idle from 1979, when the last tenant sold his stock and left the sluices to silt. It took four years, a heritage grant and a great deal of volunteer carpentry to bring the wheel back. Today it grinds about two tonnes of wheat a week, all of it grown within twelve miles of the door.

Bakers in Salisbury now queue for the wholemeal, which one of them says keeps for days and tastes of something. A second delivery goes west every Tuesday to a market stall in Truro, where the rye sells out by ten.
