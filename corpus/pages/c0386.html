6.2 The works at Barnsley

Founded1861

Workforce, 1890412 men and boys

The furnace at Quarry Lane was lit for the first time in the spring of 1861, and it was not allowed to go out for another sixty-three years. Niamh Ashworth, whose great-grandfather signed the first lease, kept the daily ledgers that survive in the town archive, and they show a works that grew by habit rather than by plan.

Each new building was added where the last had left space, so that by 1890 the site held three furnaces, two annealing sheds and a cooperage that supplied the packing crates. The ledgers record the work of the pot-setters, the gatherers and the packers in turn:

  • The glasshouse floor
    • gatherers, who took molten glass from the pot
    • blowers, who shaped the vessel
  • The finishing rooms
    • cutters and polishers
    • packers, working to the carriers' timetable
  • The yard, where coal and sand were weighed in

Output rose steadily until the 1880s. Priya Chowdhury's study of the carrier books, published in 2011, puts the yearly count of bottles at well over three million by the end of that decade, most of them sent by rail to Inverness and the northern ports. The fall after 1900 was not sudden. Stuart Odell, writing as the works' last manager, blamed the new machine-made bottle for it, but the ledgers say something more ordinary: the coal got dearer and the furnaces older, and each year the pots were mended a little more often than the year before.
