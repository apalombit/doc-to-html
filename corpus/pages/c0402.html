Measuring National Output

How do economists add up the value of everything a country produces in a year, and what does the total tell us?

12.1 Gross domestic product

Gross domestic product, or GDP, is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period, usually one year. Three words in this definition deserve attention. Market value means that goods are added up at the prices they sell for, which lets us combine apples and automobiles in one figure.

Final means that only goods sold to their last user are counted. The flour a baker buys is an intermediate good; the loaf a customer buys is final. Counting both would count the flour twice, once on its own and once inside the price of the bread.

Produced within a country means that the location of production matters, not the nationality of the owner. A car built in Ohio by a foreign-owned firm is part of American GDP.

Key idea. Every dollar spent on a final good is a dollar of income to someone. That is why output, spending and income give the same total.

12.2 The expenditure approach

The most common way to measure GDP is to add up spending on final goods by four groups of buyers: households, firms, government, and the rest of the world. This gives the national income identity:

Y = C + I + G + (X − M)

Here Y is GDP, C is consumption by households, I is investment by firms, G is government purchases, X is exports and M is imports. Net exports, X − M, can be negative when a country buys more from abroad than it sells.

Investment here means spending on new capital such as machinery, buildings and housing, plus the change in inventories. It does not mean buying shares or bonds, which only transfer ownership of existing assets.

12.3 What GDP leaves out

GDP counts only market transactions. Unpaid housework, volunteer work and the underground economy are omitted, as is the depletion of natural resources. Leisure, too, has value that no one records. For these reasons GDP is a measure of production, not of well-being, though the two usually move together.

Transfer payments such as pensions and unemployment benefits are also excluded, because nothing is produced in exchange for them. The government goods they help to buy are counted when the recipients spend the money.

12.4 Real and nominal

Table 12.1 shows GDP rising by 5.7 percent between the two years. But prices also rose, so part of this growth is only inflation. Real GDP corrects for this by valuing output at the prices of a fixed base year, and it is real GDP that economists use to judge whether an economy is growing.

The ratio of nominal to real GDP, multiplied by 100, is the GDP deflator, a broad index of prices. If nominal GDP is 21,980 and real GDP is 21,100, the deflator is 104.2, so prices have risen by about 4 percent since the base year.
