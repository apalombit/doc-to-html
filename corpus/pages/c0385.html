Estuaries and Tides

Where a river meets the sea: sediment, salinity and the rhythm of the water

An estuary is the tidal mouth of a river, where fresh water from the land mixes with salt water from the sea. In Britain, the Severn estuary has one of the largest tidal ranges in the world, and Priya Treloar's survey team at Tidewater Field Centre recorded a spring range of 12.3 m during its work upstream of Shrewsbury. Because the water is both fresh and salty, estuaries support organisms found nowhere else.

6.1 What drives the tide

Tides are caused chiefly by the gravitational pull of the Moon, and to a lesser extent the Sun. The ocean bulges towards the Moon and, on the opposite side of Earth, away from it. As Earth rotates, a coastline passes through two bulges each day, giving two high tides roughly every 24 hours and 50 minutes. When the Sun, Moon and Earth line up, the pulls add together and a spring tide follows; when they are at right angles, a smaller neap tide results.

Key idea. The tidal range is the vertical distance between high and low water. It is greatest where the shape of the coast funnels the incoming water, as in a narrowing estuary.

6.2 Mixing and mud

Where the two kinds of water meet, the denser salt water slides beneath the fresh. Fine particles carried by the river clump together in the salty water and sink, building the mudflats that fringe the channel. A single square metre (1 m2) of healthy mudflat may hold more than 40,000 mud snails, a food source for wading birds such as the redshank1.

Salt marsh forms where the mud is high enough to be covered only by the biggest tides. Plants such as glasswort tolerate being flooded and drained twice a day. Their roots trap sediment, and over decades the marsh rises. Marshes also store carbon: Owen Haworth estimated that one hectare near Hereford locks away the equivalent of 3.5 tonnes of CO2 each year2.

The discharge, Q, of a river in m3/s is its cross-sectional area times the mean velocity of its water. Fieldwork groups measure both at several points to see how the estuary narrows.
