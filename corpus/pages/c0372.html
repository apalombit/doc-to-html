Chapter 4

Grain, Stone and the Swindon Bargain

How a branch line became the quiet backbone of two towns

When the Beacon Granite Company opened its second quarry above Taunton in the spring of 1911, nobody at the board table expected the railway to matter as much as the stone. The stone was heavy, cheap to cut and, in the eyes of its directors, certain to sell. What they lacked was a way to move forty tons a day to the yards at Swindon without losing the margin to the carters. The answer, as Finlay MacLeod later put it in his memoir, was “to stop treating the line as a favour and start treating it as a customer.”

The bargain struck that winter was unglamorous. The company guaranteed a minimum tonnage each quarter; the railway promised wagons on fixed days and a rate that fell as volume rose. For the first three years neither side met its promise in full. Wagons arrived late, the quarry shipped short, and the correspondence between the two offices, which survives in boxes in the Swindon depot archive, grows steadily more polite as it grows more angry.

Rohan Odell, who audited the quarry’s books in 1927, found that the sliding rate did what it was meant to do. As tonnage rose the cost per ton fell, and the saving was split almost evenly between the two parties. Figure 4.1 sets out the quarterly tonnage shipped in the decade that followed; the shape is not a smooth climb but a staircase, each step a new customer in the south.

Not every step was earned. The dip in 1921 owes less to demand than to a strike at the quarry, and the recovery that followed was helped by a government order for kerbstones that nobody had seen coming. Historians who read the curve as a simple success story miss how much of it was luck, and how much of the luck was made by people willing to answer letters.

Taken together the figures describe a partnership that learnt, slowly and at some cost, to count in the same units. By 1926 the delays that had once filled the correspondence had fallen to a handful a year. The two towns, in the meantime, had grown into the habit of each other.
